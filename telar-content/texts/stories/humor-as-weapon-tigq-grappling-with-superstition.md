title: "grappling with superstition and class"
---

Club rhetoric often imagined working class men as particularly vulnerable to superstition. They associated masculine rationalism with capitalist prosperity--their own area of expertise as lawyers and businessmen--and decried those foolish employees who might refuse $13 wages in favor of $12.

The poem pictured here was commissioned by the Club from famed poet of the American West, Captain Jack Crawford. 


#### The Quartzville Thirteen Club
The poem, written in Crawford's signature lower-class dialect prose, begins with Southwestern men in a saloon agreeing to form a Thirteen Club of their own "jes' like those New York blokes," and ends with all 13 men experiencing absurdly bad luck and giving into superstitious belief after all. Several of the poem's victims suffer absurd injury (physical and financial), while others comedically lose their wives or girlfriends to other men. This kind of humor served ridiculed the working class men that Club members imagined as vulnerable to superstition by denying them access to classical indicators of masculine prowess: physical strength, financial prosperity, and access to women.

However, the poem's humor can also help us make sense of how the Club wished to imagine themselves. Where bad luck ruins the lives of the working-class men in Crawford's poem, it depicts the New York Club beating superstition in a "bold, defiant" physical fight. Through Crawford's poem, the Club "grappled superstition by the bosom o' the pants" and accessed the emergent ideals of muscular masculinity without soiling their black coats and top hats in a true saloon brawl. Through the poem, the Club enjoyed a vicarious taste of carnal masculinity likely alien to the medical doctors, judges, and wealthy state senators on the early member lists. 

###### muscles and manhood at the turn of the century

As Kristin Hoganson argues in her work on gender in politics, elites were distressed by the notion that their respectable professions precluded them from the opportunity to exercise a virile and violent masculinity. John Kasson makes a similar case for the performance of male muscles and physical strength in his work on White manhood at the turn of the century.

Scholars of American masculinity argue that the dramatic changes to the political economy of the late nineteenth century gave rise to a 'crisis of masculinity' among White men who agonized over what it meant to be a real man in a socio-political landscape increasingly characterized by threats from rising immigration, women's movements, and labor movements. Anthony Rotundo argues that where the ideal of the self-made man dominated early nineteenth century American culture, this archetype gave way to a masculinity characterized by mental and physical toughness in response to these stressors.

The Thirteen Club, composed almost exclusively of urban professionals in New York, were certainly conscious of the impact of immigration, labor, and women's movements. For example, prominent Club member Judge McAdam famously arbitrated several landlord disputes in favor of the working class tenants. Others, like member and printer of their reports James Kempster, would have had a vested financial interest in disputing superstitious beliefs that allegedly dissuaded their employees from working under conditions affiliated with superstition. Yet neither Club member, safe in their offices, would have any cause to exercise the new brand of muscular physical strength increasingly affiliated with true manhood at the turn of the century. In this way, Crawford's humorous take on the Quartzville Thirteen Club also exposes the New York Club elite's own insecurities.
