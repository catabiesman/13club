---
title: "What did it mean to be 'superstitious'?"
---

The club's concerns raise the question: how did they define superstition?
> "...means belief in what is unreasonable, without evidence. In regard to religious belief, the term superstition should have no application, because such belief is founded upon evidence which an intelligent world has for ages accepted as true..."

Most Club speeches reflect the view expressed here by Judge McAdam in 1884. They defined superstition as any irrational belief untethered from evidence, logic, or both. They routinely failed to apply such strict evidence demands upon their own religious beliefs, and were in fact on very good terms with many members of the Christian clergy.  

However, the Club welcomed agnostics who shared other crucial aspects of their world view. The famed orator, sometimes called The Great Agnostic, Col. Ingersoll spoke to the Club on occasion and remained an honorary member. Their differences in faith hardly mattered, because they were united by a culture of white, masculine rationalism: 
> "The only way to have brave, honest, intelligent, conscientious public men, men without superstition, is to do what we can to make the average citizen brave, conscientious and intelligent. If you wish to see courage in the presidential chair, conscience upon the bench, intelligence of the highest order in Congress; if you expect public men to be great enough to reflect honor upon the Republic, private citizens must have the courage and the intelligence to elect, and to sustain, such men."

Col. Robert Green Ingersoll warned of the dangers of superstitious beliefs to the American system of governance in a 1886 speech to the Club. His words demonstrate that while members mocked and satirized superstition, they understood the stakes of belief to be quite serious.
