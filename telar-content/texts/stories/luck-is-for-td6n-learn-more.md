In 1890, the Club's position on women's beliefs shifted in response to developments in the women's suffrage movement. Earlier internal disagreements had left the women's movement somewhat divided in the wake of the Civil War, but in 1890, two distinct organizations (the National Woman Suffrage Association and the American Woman Suffrage Association) joined forces under the new banner of National American Woman Suffrage Association.

Where before the alleged prevalence of superstition among women was charming and harmless, the growing power of the suffrage movement evidently inspired the Club to examine women's beliefs more seriously. That very year, the Club Archivist surveyed women of his acquaintance to ascertain their thoughts on superstitions. By 1891, the Club established "Ladies Nights," although in keeping with their racial and socio-economic prejudices, invitations were hardly inclusive. Furthermore, while white women of their socio-economic class merited these allegedly educational dinners, no such opportunities were extended to the other demographics the Club alleged were rife with superstition.
